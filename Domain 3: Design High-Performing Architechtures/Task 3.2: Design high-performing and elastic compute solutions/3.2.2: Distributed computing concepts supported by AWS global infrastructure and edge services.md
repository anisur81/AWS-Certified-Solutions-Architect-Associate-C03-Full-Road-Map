# Distributed computing concepts supported by AWS global infrastructure and edge services

# 3.2.2: Distributed Computing Concepts Supported by AWS Global Infrastructure and Edge Services
 

## 1. What Is Distributed Computing?

**Distributed computing** means spreading compute, storage, and networking resources across multiple physical locations so they work together as one system. Instead of relying on a single data center, a distributed system:

- **Tolerates failure** – if one component or site fails, others continue.
- **Reduces latency** – resources sit closer to users.
- **Scales horizontally** – capacity is added by adding more nodes/locations.
- **Meets compliance needs** – data can stay within chosen geographic boundaries.

AWS builds its cloud on this principle. Its global infrastructure is a layered, distributed design: **Regions → Availability Zones → Data Centers**, extended outward by **edge services** (edge locations, Local Zones, Wavelength Zones, Outposts).

---

## 2. Core Building Blocks

### 2.1 AWS Regions

A **Region** is a separate geographic area (e.g., N. Virginia, Frankfurt, Singapore, Mumbai) that contains multiple, isolated and physically separate Availability Zones.

Key facts:
- Regions are **independent** of each other. Resources and data do not automatically replicate between Regions; **you choose** where to run workloads and where to copy data.
- Each Region has its own **service endpoints**, pricing, and service availability. Not every service is available in every Region.
- Some services are **global** (e.g., IAM, Route 53, CloudFront, AWS Organizations) while most are **Regional** (e.g., EC2, RDS, Lambda, S3 buckets are created in a Region).

**Factors for choosing a Region:**

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Compliance / data sovereignty** | Laws may require data to remain in a country or region. |
| **Latency** | Choose a Region close to your users. |
| **Service availability** | New services/features may launch in select Regions first. |
| **Cost** | Pricing differs between Regions. |

### 2.2 Availability Zones (AZs)

An **Availability Zone** is one or more discrete data centers within a Region, each with **redundant power, networking, and connectivity**, housed in separate facilities.

Key facts:
- AZs within a Region are **physically separate** (meaningful distance apart, to reduce the chance a single event such as a fire or flood affects more than one) but close enough for **low-latency, high-throughput, redundant networking** between them.
- AZs are designed so that they **do not share single points of failure**.
- Each Region has **at least three AZs** in most cases (some older Regions have more, a few have fewer).
- AZs are identified as e.g. `us-east-1a`; the mapping of names to physical zones differs per account (AZ IDs like `use1-az1` are consistent).

**Distributed computing benefit:** Deploying an application across **two or more AZs** is the standard way to achieve **high availability** and **fault tolerance**. If one AZ becomes unavailable, traffic is served from the others.

### 2.3 Data Centers

Data centers are the physical facilities (servers, storage, networking) that make up AZs. Customers don't choose or access individual data centers; they work at the AZ and Region level.

### 2.4 Edge Locations and Points of Presence (PoPs)

**Edge locations** are sites in major cities worldwide used by edge services to deliver content and traffic **close to end users**. They are part of AWS **Points of Presence (PoPs)**, which also include **Regional Edge Caches**.

Key facts:
- Edge locations **far outnumber** Regions and AZs.
- They are **not** places where you launch EC2 instances; they host edge services such as caching, DNS, and traffic acceleration.
- Connected to AWS Regions through the **AWS global network backbone** — a private, high-bandwidth, redundant fiber network that is more consistent than the public internet.

## 3. Distributed Computing Concepts Mapped to AWS

### 3.1 High Availability (HA)

**Concept:** The system continues operating despite component failure, with minimal downtime.

**AWS support:**
- Multiple AZs per Region.
- **Elastic Load Balancing (ELB)** distributes traffic across instances in different AZs and performs health checks.
- **Auto Scaling** replaces unhealthy instances and spreads them across AZs.
- **Amazon RDS Multi-AZ** keeps a synchronous standby in another AZ with automatic failover.
- **Amazon S3** stores objects redundantly across multiple AZs (for most storage classes).

### 3.2 Fault Tolerance

**Concept:** The system keeps operating *without interruption* even when a component fails.

**AWS support:** AZ isolation, redundant power/network, and services designed to run across AZs (e.g., DynamoDB, S3, EFS are Regional and replicate across AZs by design).

> **Exam tip:** High availability = minimal downtime and fast recovery. Fault tolerance = no interruption at all (typically higher cost and complexity).

### 3.3 Scalability and Elasticity

**Concept:** Add or remove resources as demand changes.

**AWS support:** Auto Scaling groups can span multiple AZs; serverless services (Lambda, Fargate, DynamoDB on-demand) scale automatically; edge services absorb traffic spikes by serving cached content.

### 3.4 Low Latency and Proximity to Users

**Concept:** Reduce the physical/network distance between users and resources.

**AWS support:**
- Choose Regions near users.
- **Amazon CloudFront** caches content at edge locations.
- **AWS Global Accelerator** routes users into the AWS network at the nearest edge.
- **Local Zones, Wavelength, Outposts** place compute even closer to users or on-premises systems.

### 3.5 Disaster Recovery (DR) and Business Continuity

**Concept:** Recover from large-scale failures (e.g., an entire Region becoming unavailable).

**AWS support — use multiple Regions for:**
- **Disaster recovery strategies** (from lowest to highest cost / fastest recovery): *Backup & Restore → Pilot Light → Warm Standby → Multi-Site Active/Active*.
- **Cross-Region replication**: S3 Cross-Region Replication, DynamoDB Global Tables, Aurora Global Database, RDS cross-Region read replicas, AWS Backup cross-Region copy.
- **DNS failover** with Amazon Route 53 health checks.

### 3.6 Data Sovereignty and Compliance

**Concept:** Data must be stored/processed under specific legal jurisdictions.

**AWS support:** Data stays in the Region you select unless you move it. AWS gives you control over where content is stored, plus tools such as **AWS Artifact** (compliance reports) and encryption options (AWS KMS).

### 3.7 Decoupling and Load Distribution

**Concept:** Components communicate asynchronously so a failure in one part doesn't cascade.

**AWS support:** Amazon SQS, SNS, EventBridge, and ELB help build loosely coupled, distributed architectures across AZs.

## 4. Edge Services in Detail

### 4.1 Amazon CloudFront (Content Delivery Network)

CloudFront is AWS's **CDN**. It caches copies of static (and dynamic) content at edge locations.

**How it works:**
1. A user requests content (e.g., an image or video).
2. DNS routes the request to the nearest edge location.
3. If cached → served immediately (**cache hit**).
4. If not cached → edge fetches from a **Regional Edge Cache** or the **origin** (S3 bucket, ALB, EC2, custom HTTP server), then caches it for future requests (**cache miss**).

**Benefits:**
- Lower latency and faster page loads.
- Reduced load on origin servers.
- Built-in security: **AWS Shield Standard** (DDoS protection), **AWS WAF** integration, HTTPS/TLS, and origin access controls.
- Programmable edge: **CloudFront Functions** and **Lambda@Edge** run code close to users (e.g., URL rewrites, header manipulation, authentication).

### 4.2 AWS Global Accelerator

Global Accelerator improves availability and performance for **TCP/UDP** applications by giving you **static anycast IP addresses** that enter the AWS global network at the nearest edge location.

**Benefits:**
- Traffic travels over the AWS backbone instead of congested public internet paths.
- **Automatic failover** to healthy endpoints across AZs or Regions (typically within seconds).
- Works with ALB, NLB, EC2 instances, and Elastic IPs.
- Good for gaming, IoT, VoIP, and non-HTTP workloads, or when fixed IPs are required.

**CloudFront vs Global Accelerator:**

| Feature | CloudFront | Global Accelerator |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Cache & deliver content | Optimize network path to apps |
| Protocols | HTTP/HTTPS (and WebSocket) | TCP, UDP |
| Caching | Yes | No |
| IP addresses | Dynamic | 2 static anycast IPs |
| Typical use | Websites, video, APIs | Gaming, VoIP, multi-Region failover |

### 4.3 Amazon Route 53 (DNS at the Edge)

Route 53 is a highly available, scalable **DNS** service running from a global network of edge locations. It supports routing policies that enable distributed architectures:

- **Latency-based routing** – send users to the lowest-latency Region.
- **Geolocation / Geoproximity routing** – route by user location (useful for compliance or localization).
- **Failover routing** – active/passive using health checks.
- **Weighted routing** – split traffic for testing or gradual migration.
- **Multi-value answer** – return multiple healthy records.

### 4.4 AWS Shield and AWS WAF at the Edge

Both can be deployed at CloudFront / edge points to **filter malicious traffic close to the source**, protecting origins from DDoS and web exploits.

## 5. Extending AWS Infrastructure Beyond Regions

### 5.1 AWS Local Zones

- Extensions of a Region placed in large metropolitan areas.
- Run select services (EC2, EBS, ECS/EKS, etc.) with **single-digit millisecond latency** to nearby users.
- **Use cases:** real-time gaming, live video streaming, media & entertainment production, AR/VR, machine-learning inference near users.
- You **opt in** to a Local Zone and extend a VPC subnet into it.

### 5.2 AWS Wavelength Zones

- AWS compute and storage embedded **inside telecommunications providers' 5G networks**.
- Traffic from 5G devices reaches applications without leaving the carrier network, giving **ultra-low latency**.
- **Use cases:** connected vehicles, smart factories, mobile gaming, live video, AR/VR.

### 5.3 AWS Outposts

- AWS-designed infrastructure (racks or servers) installed **in your own data center or on-premises facility**, managed by AWS.
- Provides a **consistent hybrid experience** with the same APIs, tools, and services.
- **Use cases:** low-latency access to on-premises systems, local data processing, data residency requirements, migration staging.

### Comparison Table

| Service | Where it runs | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Region / AZ** | AWS data centers | General workloads, HA, DR |
| **Edge location** | Major cities (PoPs) | Caching, DNS, acceleration |
| **Local Zone** | Metro areas, extension of a Region | Single-digit ms latency in cities |
| **Wavelength Zone** | Inside 5G carrier networks | Ultra-low latency mobile apps |
| **Outposts** | Customer premises | Hybrid, on-premises, data residency |

## 6. The AWS Global Network

AWS operates a **private global network backbone** that interconnects Regions, AZs, and edge locations:

- Built with redundant, high-capacity fiber, including undersea cables.
- Provides **consistent latency and throughput** compared to the public internet.
- Traffic between AWS Regions on the backbone is **encrypted** at the physical layer.
- Underpins CloudFront, Global Accelerator, S3 Transfer Acceleration, and inter-Region replication.

## 7. Putting It Together: Reference Architectures

### 7.1 Highly Available Web Application (Single Region)

```
Users → Route 53 → CloudFront (edge) → ALB (multi-AZ)
                                         ├─ EC2 / Auto Scaling in AZ-a
                                         ├─ EC2 / Auto Scaling in AZ-b
                                         └─ EC2 / Auto Scaling in AZ-c
                                      → RDS Multi-AZ (primary + standby)
```

- Survives the loss of an entire AZ.
- Static assets are cached at the edge, reducing origin load.

### 7.2 Multi-Region Disaster Recovery / Global Application

```
Users → Route 53 (latency / failover routing)
        ├─ Region A: ALB + app tier + Aurora primary
        └─ Region B: ALB + app tier + Aurora Global Database replica
(Global Accelerator can front both Regions for static IPs and fast failover)
```

- Survives a full Region outage.
- Serves users from the nearest Region for lower latency.
- Helps meet data sovereignty by selecting appropriate Regions.

## 8. Shared Responsibility and Customer Choices

- **AWS is responsible for:** the physical infrastructure — data centers, power, cooling, network backbone, AZ/Region isolation.
- **You are responsible for:** architecting for resilience — choosing Regions, deploying across multiple AZs, configuring replication, backups, failover, and security of your data and applications.

Simply running in AWS does **not** automatically make your workload highly available. You must **design** for it.

---

## 9. Key Takeaways

1. **Regions** are isolated geographic areas; **AZs** are isolated data-center groups inside a Region with no shared single points of failure.
2. **Multi-AZ** deployments provide **high availability**; **multi-Region** deployments provide **disaster recovery, lower global latency, and data sovereignty** options.
3. **Edge locations** (CloudFront, Route 53, Global Accelerator, Shield, WAF) bring services close to end users to **reduce latency** and absorb traffic and attacks.
4. **CloudFront** = caching CDN (HTTP). **Global Accelerator** = static anycast IPs and network optimization (TCP/UDP).
5. **Local Zones, Wavelength Zones, and Outposts** extend AWS infrastructure to metros, 5G networks, and on-premises sites for ultra-low latency or hybrid needs.
6. The **AWS global network backbone** connects everything with consistent performance.
7. Customers choose Regions based on **compliance, latency, service availability, and cost**.
8. Resilience is a **shared responsibility**: AWS provides the infrastructure; you design the architecture.
